Billing Ablutions. Chapter 1. Corporate: Internal.

A Mr. Stoepker of Queens, New York went furiously, clompingly, around his apartment on the morning in question muttering “my keys” under his breath. “My keys,” he muttered as he dipped his head under the bed. “My keys,” he muttered stooping toward the floor under the coat hooks. “My keys,” he said as if making a grand reveal as he pulled out the pockets of the coat on the coat hook. Emptyhanded, he even went to the vegetable shelf of the refrigerator presumably looking for his keys. Mr. Stoepker’s daily Rent Ablutions went out early in the morning just as he was fondling the broccoli, and it may have been one of these co-occurrences that caused him to lift his head, shake it once or twice, and go to tie his tie around his neck. However, the internal causes of his actions go beyond the scope of this memo.

Mr. Stoepker whispered under his breath “wide end on the right” as he arranged his tie around his white collar and looked himself squarely in the mirror: an upright, smiling financial manager with an edgy goatee and a day’s worth of facial hair on the rest of his facial template.

Several months before, just a couple weeks after his partner had left him, Mr. Stoepker had been sitting in the oﬃce jangling his keys in his pocket. A junior employee came up behind him intending to schmooze but found Mr. Stoepker with his hand rustling in his pants and, though there were no words exchanged or even wide-eyed looks, Mr. Stoepker’s face flushed, and he sat there looking up at the junior analyst with his hand seized inside his pocket.

It might have been this incident that Mr. Stoepker thought of as he checked his appearance in the mirror, because his face was red just like it had been back then, and he looked in no more spots in his apartment. He must have thought this event was the day before and that was why he must have left his keys in the oﬃce. If that was what he was thinking about, which is a matter of speculation, that would of course be a mixup about the timing of the occurrence, but he would have been right in thinking that his keys were left at the oﬃce.

Mr. Stoepker had only managed to get into his apartment the evening before by calling up to his mother on the fourth floor of the walk-up building. After tapping his dead phone and pacing the sidewalk, he called up first at a near whisper, “Hey, Mama,” then with much more vigor, “Hey Janice, let me in!” His voice died down in the echoey space between buildings. “I lost my keys.”

A small woman stuck her head out the window next to the one with the air conditioner, slowly and timidly as if she thought she might get shot at by a strange man who called for her out of the dark. Once she saw her big, grown boy on the street though, she called back, “Okay, Bill. Just a minute.” She made her way downstairs, slowly but with great resolve, as she always did at least twice, but not more than four times per day. At this time of night, it was her fourth. Thanks to his mama, Mr. Stoepker made it up to his bed for a night’s rest, albeit tossing because he had lost his keys. Mr. Stoepker’s mother moved in with him shortly after his partner left him, but he told others when they cared to ask about his living situation that her health reasons were the cause for the move-in. At the time she had a sprained ankle and a heart diagnosis. Whether what he said was true, that it was for her sake and not his that she lived with him, would be considered a fuzzy question and a far more interior topic than this memo is concerned with.

More pertinent is the fact that Mr. Stoepker’s daily Rent Ablutions, paid simultaneously with his broccoli fondling, went directly to the Corpo- ration, and the Corporation was then entrusted with remittance of 97% to Mr. Stoepker’s landlord and taking the rest as an operating fee. Mr. Stoepker stepped out of his and his mother’s apartment without his keys and sighed after the door closed behind him. Then, quietly, with a chivalrous erect spine, he walked down the flights of stairs to the ground level.

The street where his building sat was quiet and dark blue, lit by humming streetlights. A beeper-less forklift delivered pallets of liquor and cigarettes from the back of an idling truck to the one store on the block. Mr. Stoepker took a right onto a larger street which led to the subway station. He did not look up at a sign above the sidewalk that read: ``Presence Toll Ahead, Next Five Blocks. Keep Left to Avoid Billing Ablutions.'' The left side of the sidewalk was much more crowded, and it may have been for this reason that Mr. Stoepker blindly gave his tacit consent to Presence Tolling by stepping over the freshly painted white line. After ten minutes of walking, he started going down into the subway, and thirty cents went from his bank account to the Corporation.

The frictionless transaction executed on Mr. Stoepker’s behalf had its origins in what has been coined “Zero Settlement,” which only just recently went into eﬀect in late 2031. This was the Y2K event that really happened, a grumbled-about switch that got flipped in late 2031. The diﬀerence between Y2K and Zero Settlement is first, that Zero Settlement happened and second, that Zero Settlement really was a positive development rather than a negative one. Transactions reached a frictionless singularity of sorts. A full history of Zero Settlement goes beyond the scope of this memo, but it is worth noting that the transition did not go without conflict. The transition was technically feasible five years before, but it was caught up with all sorts of opposition from lawmakers, economists, and regulatory agencies. One article from a second-rate news outlet quoted a higher-up at the Federal Trade Commission as saying, “The deployment of Zero Settlement as cur- rently conceived would inevitably lead to a flood of transactions obscured in their meaning from the consumer.” That was really all there ever was to the resistance to Zero Settlement. To be clear, the argument did not, and does not, have any bearing on the everyday consumer though. It doesn’t even make sense to anyone without a joint degree in philosophical semiotics and microeconomics. After all, who cares about the meaning of transactions? Consumers pay for things they get. There’s no need to fluﬀ up the concepts. Mr. Stoepker’s account credited thirty cents to the Corporation for use of of the express lane of the sidewalk for ten minutes. On the subway, he listened to a rock song about vampiric businessmen. The subway car was filled with standers and sitters as it plunged underground, leaving a view of tall Manhattan behind, and he continued his commute until he rolled into Wall Street Station.

He went first to the security desk in the basement of the skyscraper. Above the turnstiles a sign read: “Billing Ablutions Presence Toll. Minimize Lobby Time to Minimize Toll.” Mr. Stoepker did not look up at the sign, but he did move eﬃciently among the throng toward the bedrock on which stood, in other buildings nearby, racks upon racks and plastic-wrapped pallet crates of gold bars. In this bedrock basement there was a security desk and a lost and found.

A security agent sat at the desk laughing at something on her phone. “Hi,” Mr. Stoepker said. “I’m looking for a set of keys I think I left in the oﬃce yesterday.”

The agent looked up from her phone and scowled at him. “What oﬃce is that?” she asked.

He said the name of his company.

“I don’t think we got any of those from there,” she said.

“I’m sorry to bug you, but do you think you could check for it?”

“Anything from that oﬃce would go in this bin here,” she said, spinning around on her tall oﬃce chair and pointing at a clear basket with wires peaking out.

“There’s nothing in there at all, especially no keys.”

“Especially no keys,” he repeated. Could you check in the places where it would go if it was from another floor?”

The security agent gave a heavy sigh. “You’re asking me to check if it got sorted wrong?”

“Yeah, if someone put it in the wrong bin maybe.”

She stuck the chunky phone in her back pocket then disappeared into a back room. Mr. Stoepker tapped his fingers on the desk while he waited alone at the security desk for six minutes.

Bursting back through the door she said, “No keys back here. None from any of the seventy floors.”

He thanked her for checking, then took oﬀ to his own oﬃce floor, at which point time stopped accruing and a debit of $3.75 went to the Corporation for use of lobby and security desk areas.

Still before nine o’clock, Mr. Stoepker pounced on his hot-seat workstation from the day before, swiped his hand across the desktop, around the spongy keyboard and mouse, underneath the dual monitors. He lifted a pad of sticky notes and shook a box of tissues upside down. He leaned down to look at the floor at the edges of the desk. He winced when his right knee bent, so he kept it straight behind him as he pawed around the base of the desk.

“Bill,” said a resonant voice behind him.

Mr. Stoepker stiﬀened, pushed his shirttail back in his pants, came to standing, then turned around with a tightlipped professional smile. It was Mike, the junior analyst with a resonant voice he adopted at the oﬃce which sounded uncannily like the VP. “How you doin’?” Mike said.

“Mike.” Mr. Stoepker’s face softened. “Sorry, I thought you were someone else.”

“You see this weather today?”

“Yeah, I was in it.”

“Crazy, huh?”

“Is it that crazy?”

“The rain is from a hurricane. In early December.”

“Maybe that is crazy,” Mr. Stoepker said. He went back to rummaging in the trash can.

“Is there anything I can help you with?”

“Have you seen my keys?”

“I haven’t, but I’ll keep an eye out.”

“Thanks.” Mr. Stoepker kept stooping to the trash can, then to the next one.

Mike kept standing there. “I think you’re in my desk for today.”

“Right you are,” he said, and went to his own desk across the floor.

The keys jangled on the heater vent no more loudly than the brown noise that whooshed through it.

At lunchtime he went to the cafeteria where he had gone for lunch the day before and asked the cashier if a set of keys had turned up.

“No, we don’t have that,” the cashier said. “Are you going to buy anything?”

“I brought my lunch today,” he said.

“I just have to inform you that this restaurant has enrolled in the Billing Ablutions Presence Toll program, so if you don’t buy anything you will be tolled.”

“Told what? And what presents do I get?” He chuckled to himself but the cashier looked exasperated.

“You will be tolled if you do not buy anything.”

“Whatever you say,” said Mr. Stoepker, and he walked out of the cafeteria restaurant.

At the moment he walked out the door, a debit of $5.04 went to the Corporation for remittance to the cafeteria restaurant.

He returned to his desk and ate his mother’s sandwich in peace.

“Mr. Stoepker,” rang out a resonant voice behind him.

“Mike,” he said still facing the screen. He recognized the voice now. “I’m busy now. You’ll have to ping me on chat like the guys from the other oﬃces do.”

“I just thought you’d want to know I found your keys.” Mike jangled them, ringing Mr. Stoepker’s ears.

“My keys!” Mr. Stoepker grabbed them from Mike’s freely outstretched hand. “Where did you find them?”

“Well, I had some time between meetings, so I combed through some likely spots. I ended up finding them on the heating vent. Can you believe it? Just hanging into the air shaft through the grate, nice and toasty. The whole thing would have fallen through if you didn’t have so many keys on there.”

“That’s great,” said Mr. Stoepker, “but you really didn’t need to spend your own time at work looking for my keys.”

“Oh, it’s no problem. I knocked out a lot of reportage this morning.”

“That’s good.” Stoepker took another bite of his mother’s sandwich.

Mike’s smile turned serious, his brow furrowed, and his voice bright crimson. “It is going to cost you though,” he said. Mr. Stoepker’s face stiﬀened. A swallow of soft bread caught in his throat, and his goatee sank along with his jaw. “Just kidding,” said Mike. Mr. Stoepker smiled back, then returned to eating his mother’s sandwich followed by an afternoon in the oﬃce.

The recovered key opened the front door to Mr. Stoepker’s building, but before he could insert the key into his apartment door, his mother swung it wide open. He towered over her on the outside of the doorframe. Inside it was warm, dry, and dimly lit.

“Did you find your keys?” she asked with a hopeful look.

“Why don’t you close the door and find out?” He jangled the set in front of her face.

“Oh, how could I close the door on my own son?” Without warning, she grabbed him and pulled his cheek down to kiss. “You didn’t shave this morning, did you?”

“I had had to leave early to look for my keys. Didn’t have time.”

“You’ll shave tomorrow, won’t you?”

“Of course, Ma. You know I almost always do.”

“Good. You’ve got to keep that goatee of yours looking distinctive.” She looked at him with beaming black eyes.

“I still want to try it,” said Mr. Stoepker, stepping back outside.

“Okay, suit yourself.” She closed the door on him with a thud. He slipped his key into the keyhole and opened it back up. His mama was still right there. They both smiled.

Once Mr. Stoepker crossed the threshold to his and his mother’s apartment, time stopped accruing and forty cents was transferred to the Corporation for remittance of 97% to his landlord for use of the common areas of the building.

He slept more much more soundly than he had the night before. His heart rate variability was elevated throughout the night, and there was only one small interruption to his sleep at the end of a rapid eye movement cycle when he got up to use the bathroom. Not knowing where his keys were must have weighed on him much more heavily than he or anyone was aware. The next morning, he shaved his whiskers and mustache, and at the moment when he removed two stray hairs from his Adam’s apple, his daily Rent Ablutions went to the Corporation for remittance of 97% to his landlord.
